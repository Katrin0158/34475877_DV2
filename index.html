A century of cinema: where films come from and what makes them pay.

An investigation into 4,794 films from The Movie Database, released between 1916 and 2017.

It covers countries, genres, ratings, studios and box office.

01 · WHERE FILMS ARE MADE AND MONEY MOVES

WHERE FILMS ARE MADE

Three maps show who makes films, who earns from them and who works together. Together they show how concentrated cinema is.

Choropleth map

Most films come from a handful of countries

Countries are shaded by films made per million people, so big and small countries compare fairly. Darker means more.

Films per million people (2016 population).

  • 3,952 films the US helped make, out of 4,794. That is 12.2 films per million people.
  • 9.7 films per million people in the UK. Ireland (7.8) and Canada (7.2) follow.
  • 2 films in total list Malaysia as a producer, about 0.06 per million people.

Proportional symbol map

Box office follows the same few countries

Each bubble is a country, sized by the total takings of its films.

Move the slider to change the final year. Hover over a bubble for its numbers.

Total box office of films with revenue data, in US dollars (not adjusted for inflation).

  • 69% of the combined country totals comes from films the US helped make (US$380 billion).
  • US$60B for the UK, then Germany (US$25B) and Canada (US$15B).

Flow map

The UK and US make the most films together

Each line joins two countries that made a film together. Thicker lines mean more joint films.

Move the slider to show only the strongest partnerships.

Lines join country centres, not routes travelled.

  • 378 films made jointly by the UK and US, the strongest link by far.
  • 249 for Germany and the US, then Canada and the US with 160.
  • 13 partnerships have 30 or more joint films. That is what the map shows at first.

02 · WHAT GENRES THE FILMS BELONG TO

GENRE PROFILES

How genres grew, how audiences rate them and which ones mix.

Streamgraph

Drama leads every decade

Each band is one genre. The wider the band, the more films were released.

Click a genre in the legend to highlight it.

A film can belong to several genres. The 2010s end in early 2017, so that band is shorter.

  • 928 drama films in the 2000s, up from 39 in the 1960s.
  • 451 action films in the 2000s, against 16 in the 1960s.
  • 513 thrillers in the 2000s, against 14 in the 1960s.

Heatmap

Documentaries and war films score highest

Each square is the average rating of one genre in one decade. Darker means higher.

Blue cells mean no data: fewer than 3 films in that genre and decade.

  • 6.9 average rating for documentaries, the highest of any genre since 1950.
  • 5.7 for horror, the lowest of the main genres. Horror in the 2010s averages 5.5.
  • 7.5 for comedies in the 1950s, but from only 4 films, so treat it with care.

Matrix chart

Drama is in three of the five most common pairings

Each square counts the films that carry both genres. Darker means more.

All 4,794 films are included.

  • 602 films combine drama and romance, the most common pairing.
  • 574 combine comedy and drama.
  • 552 combine drama and thriller, just ahead of action and thriller (547).

03 · HOW FILMS CHANGED OVER TIME

CHANGE ACROSS THE YEARS

Ratings, film length and release timing have all shifted.

Density plot

Average ratings have drifted down since the 1950s

Each curve shows how ratings spread across one era. Further right means better-rated films.

A taller curve means more films at that rating.

  • 7.2 → 6.1 average rating in the 1950s and in the 2010s.
  • 9% of 2010s films are rated below 5, against 1% of films before 1970.
  • Survivors Old films we still watch are the ones people kept loving. Modern ratings include every release.

Line chart

Films peaked at 128 minutes in the 1960s

The line shows the typical (median) length of films in each decade.

The axis starts at zero so the change is not exaggerated.

  • 128 minutes in the 1960s, the longest typical film.
  • 104 minutes in the 2010s.
  • 102 minutes in the 1930s (101.5 exactly), close to today.

Vertical Bar chart

June is the best month to release a film

Bars show the typical (median) box office of films released in each month.

Films from 1990 onwards.

  • US$149M typical takings for films released in June.
  • US$108M for December, the second-best month.
  • US$28M for September, the weakest month.

04 · FOLLOWING THE MONEY

BUDGET AND BOX OFFICE

What films cost, what they earn and who profits.

Scatter plot

Big budgets don't guarantee big profits

Each dot is one film. Dots above the dashed line earned more than they cost.

Click an era in the legend to highlight it.

Both axes start at 0 and use a square-root scale so small and large films fit together. Not adjusted for inflation.

  • US$2.7M typical budget in the 1950s, against US$30M from 1990 onwards.
  • 24% of films with budget data earned less than they cost.

Box plot

Higher-rated films earn more, but not always

Films are grouped by audience rating. Each box covers the middle half of films.

Square-root scale starting at 0. The label marks the typical (median) film.

  • US$77M typical box office for films rated above 7.5, against US$33M for films rated under 5.5.
  • US$61M for films rated 5.5 to 6.5. Films rated 6.5 to 7.5 earn a little less, US$55M.
  • Big overlap the middle half of films rated above 7.5 earn US$20M to US$286M. For films under 5.5 it is US$13M to US$76M.

Radar chart

Documentaries earn the most for every dollar spent

Each corner is one measure, scaled so 100 is the highest of all 18 genres. Compare how far each genre's shape reaches.

Hover over a dot to see the real value.

Only 32 documentaries have budget data, so treat that shape with care.

  • 3.9 times typical return for documentaries, on a typical budget of US$3M.
  • 2.9 times for horror, on a typical budget of US$14M.
  • 2.8 times for animation, which spends most, US$75M per film.

Lollipop chart

Warner Bros. leads the biggest studios

Each dot is a studio, placed by its total box office. Darker dots mean more films.

The 15 biggest studios, each with at least 15 films that have revenue data.

  • 280 films for Warner Bros., the most of any studio here.
  • 272 for Universal Pictures, then Paramount (242).
  • 5 studios have more than 160 films each in this collection.

Diverging bar chart

Avatar made the most money. The Lone Ranger lost the most.

Profit is box office minus budget. The eight biggest winners are orange and the eight biggest losers dark slate.

Profit in US dollars, not adjusted for inflation. Only films with both budget and revenue data.

  • US$2.55B profit for Avatar, then Titanic (US$1.65B) and Jurassic World (US$1.36B).
  • −US$166M loss for The Lone Ranger, then The Alamo (−US$119M).
