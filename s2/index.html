A century of cinema: where films come from and what makes them pay

Which countries make the movies we watch? Which genres earn the most for every ringgit spent? And have films really got worse? Scroll through three maps and eight more charts to find out.

Where in the world

Choropleth Map

Films per million people (2016 population). Hover over a country for details.

The United States helped make 3,952 of the 4,794 films in this collection, more than five times the next country, the United Kingdom (635). Adjust for population and the picture gets more interesting: Ireland and Canada punch well above their weight.

Malaysia appears in only 2 films here, about 0.06 per million people. Our own industry is almost invisible in global film databases.

How taste changed

Genres rise and fall by decade

The band for each genre widens when more films of that type were released. Drama has led every decade, while action and thrillers grew from a handful of films in the 1950s to hundreds from the 1990s onwards. The 2010s only run to early 2017, so that band is shorter.

Number of films per genre, by decade (a film can belong to several genres). Top 8 genres, 1950s onwards. Click a genre in the legend to highlight it.

Audiences rate genres differently too. The darker the gold, the higher the average rating. Documentaries, war and history films are rated highest on average, and horror lowest.

Average audience rating (out of 10) by genre and decade.

Have films got worse?

Ratings drifted down, but the reason is surprising

The average rating fell from about 7.2 in the 1950s to about 6.1 in the 2010s. Old films we still watch today are the survivors that people kept loving, while modern ratings include everything that gets released, good or bad. The curves below show that recent films are spread across a wider range of ratings.

Distribution of audience ratings by era. A taller curve means more films at that rating.

Following the money

Big budgets don't guarantee big profits

The typical film cost about US$2.9 million in the 1950s and about US$30 million from the 1990s onwards (not adjusted for inflation). But spending more does not always pay: on the chart, any dot below the dashed line lost money.

Each dot is one film. The dashed line marks break-even (revenue equals budget). Click an era in the legend to highlight it.

The best return for money spent comes from small films. The typical documentary earns about 3.9 times its budget and the typical horror film about 2.9 times, compared with only 1.8 times for westerns and 1.9 times for history films. (Only 32 documentaries and 56 westerns have budget data, so treat those two with care.) The radar below shows the trade-off: documentaries and horror films cost little and earn back the most for each dollar, while animation spends the most (about US$75 million for a typical film) and takes the most at the box office, yet returns only about 2.8 times its cost.

Each corner is one measure, and the further a genre's shape reaches towards a corner, the higher it scores. 100 means the highest of all 18 genres and 0 the lowest. Hover over a dot for the real value.

Five of the top studios each have more than 160 films with financial data in this collection. Warner Bros. leads on number of films, with 280.

Small details

Timing and length

Summer and the year-end holidays are the best times to release a film: June has the highest median takings of any month (about US$149 million) and December follows (about US$108 million). September is the weakest at about US$28 million.

Median box office by release month, films from 1990 onwards.

Films peaked in length in the 1960s, at a median of 128 minutes, and have settled around 104 minutes since.

Median film length by decade, 1930s onwards.
